Designing Instagram posts, administering a WhatsApp group, coordinating Zoom meetings…these are not activities that come first in mind when thinking about volunteer work, right?

Yet, it is what more and more people end up spending their time doing when joining charities, especially students. This gap between why people join charities—usually wanting to help impoverished persons directly with on-the-ground actions—and what they actually do in them—various digital activities—can lead to acute feelings of disconnection and disappointment, deteriorating one’s willingness to engage in social work; this paradox, related to the digitalisation of the workplace, is the core issue that underpins our study.

Research question

How has the digitalisation of organisational work and on-the-ground actions reshaped and impacted volunteer experience in nonprofit social welfare organisations?

This interrogation will lead us to delve into the complex reconfigurations these organisations have been undergoing with the advent of the digital world at the turn of the 21st century, focusing on Sciences Po Menton campus’ societies landscape.

Working hypotheses

Linked to broader sociological concepts of managerialisation and marketisation of the workplace, we will underscore how this digital turn has impacted the solidarity sector and its goods and services—which have long been regarded as lying outside the market logic.

Our hypothesis will be twofold.

1) We will demonstrate the detrimental effects of digital tools on social work experience

Marketisation & managerialisation patterns1

There is a crucial paradox: nonprofit social welfare organisations’ mission is to counteract the harms of the market logic—capitalism in general—yet, they end up importing that same logic through the tools they use—being built by and for corporate efficiency, they carry a commodifying rationality.

Eg: Customer Relationship Management software (CRM)—initially built for sales teams—are sometimes used to “track engagement” and suddenly beneficiaries and volunteers are treated as a record to be tracked and optimized.

Ethics under pressure for efficiency

Charities are put under pressure for efficiency and thereby adopt digital tools, yet, it comes with substantial costs to their ethics: environmental costs (water/energy use of AI systems) and privacy costs (beneficiary data on external, marketing-oriented platforms) while claiming to be part of a values-driven, non-exploitative sector.

Eg: a charity using a free AI writing tool to draft grant applications or outreach messages about vulnerable populations, without asking where that beneficiary data or the AI company’s supply chain sits ethically.

Alienation from the cause

Social workers are heavily impacted by these transformations: the more work shifts are dedicated to digital tools instead of face-to-face outreach, the more workers might report a disconnect from the reason they joined. Following Marx’s alienation concept, they lose connection to the person the labor is supposed to help, as well as a sense of collective consciousness with their colleagues.

Eg: volunteers spend more time scheduling shifts on a Google Sheet than serving meals at a shelter — technically “helping,” but no longer seeing it.

Loss of the solidarity spirit

The more the work resembles “a job like any other”—logged hours, standardized workflows—the more it risks losing the informal, human, non-transactional spirit that distinguishes solidarity work from paid labor. This can even open the door to informally ranking beneficiaries (who’s “worth” the time/resources) in ways that erode the inclusiveness the organization was built on.

Eg: a food bank’s intake software auto-flagging “high-need” vs. “low-need” cases for volunteer efficiency—quietly triaging people the way a business triages customers.

2) However, our analysis would not be complete without mentioning the nuanced and sometimes beneficial aspects of this digital revolution

Enhanced efficiency and sense of mission-fulfillment

Digital coordination tools let smaller volunteer bases cover more ground than they physically could otherwise—and workers often report feeling their limited time is better spent when logistics are handled well.

Eg: a shelter using a shared scheduling app—rather than endless phone calls—so that every shift is covered, meaning volunteers actually spend their in-person time with residents instead of resolving scheduling chaos.

Lower barriers to entry and broader volunteer recruitment

Digital tools let organizations recruit volunteers who couldn’t otherwise participate—people with limited mobility, time-zone constraints, or those juggling jobs/studies—expanding who gets to do solidarity work at all.

Eg: a student association running hybrid meetings so members with part-time jobs can still contribute remotely (translation, design, admin) instead of being excluded entirely.

Knowledge-sharing and reduced burnout

Shared digital documentation (case notes, best practices, training materials) means new volunteers ramp up faster and do not have to reinvent work practices alone—which can reduce the isolation and burnout that’s common in frontline and emotionally loaded work.

Eg: a peer-support hotline using a shared, searchable resource so a new volunteer facing a difficult call at 2am isn’t improvising alone.

Data-driven advocacy and legitimacy

Digital tools let small organizations produce the kind of impact data (reports, dashboards, outcome tracking) that funders, media, and policymakers respond to—giving grassroots work a louder, more credible voice than word-of-mouth alone could.

Eg: a local association using tracking data (“we distributed X meals, reached Y people”) to secure a municipal grant that funds an extra staff position for ground-level work.

What the literature says

Research on technology and social work is growing fast, but it mostly examines paid professionals and large organisations.

We look at three studies that go in different directions: one is mostly critical, another is mixed and the third is more positive.

Behind all three studies lies a debate about the marketisation of the nonprofit sector. The debate worries that charities which adopt business methods and tools slowly take on business values too (Eikenberry & Kluver, 2004; Hwang & Powell, 2009).

The critical view: from street-level to screen-level work

Johansson (2025) reviewed 168 peer-reviewed articles published between 1999 and 2023 on the topic of “digital discretion”. These articles explore how digital technology changes the space for judgement that frontline public workers have.

The main finding is that street-level workers become “screen-level” or “system-level” bureaucrats. Instead of judging the case in front of them, they feed information into a system that frames the decision for them.

Johansson calls this effect “subjectification”. Digital technology changes not only what workers do, but also how they are expected to see themselves. It pushes them to monitor their performance and adapt to the system’s logic.

Much of the literature reviewed uses metaphors, such as the “digital cage”, “digital sclerosis” or Weber’s iron cage made stronger. These metaphors are the academic language closest to our marketisation and managerialisation hypothesis.

The review also points to a risk for the people being helped. When clients are anonymised so that decisions can be standardised, the particulars of each case are veiled. The individual vulnerability that a face-to-face encounter would catch disappears. The literature calls this a form of “epistemic injustice”, which gives a theoretical name to the kind of triage in the food bank example.

Still, the review is not one-sided. Some studies describe technology as a “colleague, not a cage” that supports workers’ judgement rather than replacing it.

The mixed view: beneficiaries talking back

Dinh and O’Leary (2025) shift the focus to beneficiaries. Studying the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), they show how social media lets people affected by crises demand accountability outside the organisation’s formal channels.

Their examples are concrete: Syrians in besieged areas told the ICRC online that education mattered more to them than food, and aid changed accordingly; residents of Aleppo used digital channels to report broken water points.

One interviewee describes beneficiaries becoming “semi-consumers” who compare NGOs the way one compares prices, a shift away from organisations deciding people’s needs on their own.

The authors do not see this as simple empowerment. Accountability now runs through platforms that act as gatekeepers and shape which voices are heard.

They acknowledge critiques of “data colonialism” and recognise that they cannot confirm that all affected populations have equal access. This is a warning about digital exclusion.

Greater visibility also created new duties for the organisation: protecting biometric data and managing the “digital trails” left by the people it serves.

The positive view, with conditions: autonomy and burnout

Gnugesser and colleagues (2026) challenge the alienation argument directly. Surveying 906 counsellors in a large German welfare organisation, they found that those who did more of their counselling online reported higher autonomy, not lower.

The benefit was not direct. Autonomy only improved health and reduced burnout through better work-life balance, and by softening specific pressures such as interruptions and difficult working environments.

Their conclusion is that the effects of technology depend on context.

Interestingly, two thirds of their respondents spent 10% or less of their working month on digital counselling. This reminds us that even in professional social work, digitalisation is uneven.

For us, this study matters as a lesson in method as much as a finding: the field expects conditional, nuanced answers, not a flat verdict for or against digital technology.

The gap our study addresses

None of these studies looks at unpaid volunteers in small local organisations. Johansson and Gnugesser study paid public-sector and welfare professionals; Dinh and O’Leary study one of the largest humanitarian organisations in the world.

Student societies differ on every count: no salaries, no private funders, high turnover and members who grew up online.

That is the gap our case study addresses: do the dynamics these studies describe still hold at the scale of a campus society?

Case study and key findings

The goal of our case study is to assess how the rather macro-level dynamics that non-profit social organisations are undergoing—as we’ve highlighted in our arguments and literature review—can be reproduced, or not, in micro-level, smaller organisations. We focused on the various campus societies in Sciences Po Menton.

Method

Individual, semi-structured interviews.

Aim

Identify the importance of on-the-ground actions in interviewed students’ volunteering time vs. digital activities, in order to unearth digital tools’ role in shaping this experience.

Sample

Three second-year students involved in campus societies that provide on-the-ground activities focused on societal issues (poverty, dialogue, racism, etc.) and who are part of more than one division within them.

Taken together, they cover more than 6 societies on campus (from approximately 40 to 100+ members), and have already had a year of experience in them.

Our interview guide (14 questions)
  1. Which societies are you in, and which takes most of your time or matters most to you?
  2. What’s your role there?
  3. How did you first hear about the society and get involved?
  4. Why did you join, and what did you picture your involvement looking like?
  5. Walk me through last week specifically. What did you do for the society, and roughly how long did each thing take?
  6. Which digital tools does the society use? Are any paid, and who pays? Has anyone ever calculated the time they take?
  7. Which of your digital tasks feel connected to the cause, and which feel disconnected from it? Why?
  8. Tell me about a moment you felt most connected to why you joined, and one where you felt furthest from it.
  9. Could the things you do online be done face-to-face instead? What would be gained or lost?
  10. Do digital tasks eat into your on-the-ground time and energy, or do they make that time more effective?
  11. How do beneficiaries contact or find the society? Have you noticed anyone being missed because they aren’t reachable online?
  12. Do you store any information about beneficiaries on these platforms (WhatsApp, Sheets, Instagram)? Do you use AI tools like ChatGPT for society work? Has anyone discussed whether that’s okay?
  13. Does working mostly through group chats and shared docs change how you relate to other members? Is there shared documentation that helps new people, or do they figure things out alone?
  14. How would you describe your overall feeling about digital tools in your society work? (Asked open first, then with these options.)
    1. It feels alienating because I sometimes forget what the real purpose of my work is
    2. It feels constraining because it takes up much time and energy that I would prefer to use for on-the-ground actions
    3. It feels neither helping or constraining as it is a necessary component of my work
    4. It is much helping because it enables me to spend more time providing on-the-ground actions

Critical analysis: alienation as a risk, optimisation as a goal

Individual use and awareness of digital tools’ pitfalls as the key metric of adjustment. Are Sciences Po Menton campus societies validating, challenging, or nuancing our thesis?

All the consequences we identified in our first and second parts were mentioned by our interviewees, albeit in varying ways and degrees. Our case study thereby supports our thesis while nuancing it for student contexts and small-scale organisations. Not everything fits: Interviewee 1 joined precisely because digital tools were not central to her societies, and Interviewee 3 found them purely helpful—both push against our hook. Still, digital tools—mostly the Google ecosystem and WhatsApp—are omnipresent, although less so for volunteers who do not hold leading positions. No campus society has escaped the turn to digital.

Marketisation & managerialisation patterns

We can validate these patterns first: the digital tools and organisational activities used are, by essence, corporate, market-orientated creations that do not follow the original logic of nonprofit social welfare organisations. Volunteers feel this in practice: Interviewee 1 described purely administrative logistics as feeling “no different from a corporate job.” However, this managerial logic is less dominant than in international NGOs, since campus societies manage fewer volunteers and beneficiaries and do not rely on private financiers. Digitalisation mainly helps volunteers fit their engagement around university work; yet, some report feeling as though they perform “chores” rather than meaningful, solidarity work.

Ethics under pressure for efficiency

This is also a student concern. Personal information is necessarily handled on digital platforms whose treatment of data is opaque, so societies take precautions—anonymising beneficiary data, keeping confidential details off WhatsApp—to limit the commercial use of personal data.

Alienation from the cause

This was widely discussed as the main risk of digital tools. Interviewees 1 and 2 stated that, while they might not feel alienated, they are aware of the dangers of a “digital distancing”: they have to keep reminding themselves of the cause their digital work serves, or else it feels “mechanical” and unpleasant. Interviewee 3, who holds a leadership role, did not raise this risk and saw digital tools as purely enabling, while still finding communication tasks more connected to the mission than organisational ones.

Alienation seems to depend on one’s role and on the kind of digital task performed.

Loss of the solidarity spirit

These detrimental sides can ultimately lead to a loss of the solidarity spirit when most organisational tasks and meetings happen online. Face-to-face encounters were identified as essential for empathy, communication and community-building, and their digital transposition as having the opposite effect. Yet, given how embedded these tools are in students’ recreational and academic lives, a radical return to non-digital methods seems hard to imagine—a strong paradox of our epoch.

However, digital tools’ beneficial outcomes were strongly and consensually put forward.

Enhanced efficiency and sense of mission-fulfillment

Enhanced efficiency was highlighted as the main gain: these technologies were portrayed as essential for on-the-ground actions to take place at all. Rather than a strict dichotomy between “digital” and “real life,” the logic that prevails is one of mutual dependency: the same managerial patterns that bring efficiency and speed also bring alienation and ethical issues, and vice versa.

Knowledge-sharing and reduced burnout

For leadership turnovers especially, digital archives of previous years’ material were described as an important gain, enabling better services while reducing arduous labour.

Data-driven advocacy and legitimacy

Social media outreach lets societies reach their highly connected audience, students, with the kind of content they interact with. Data plays its part too: SPRH volunteers log the number of people they meet at the border by category in Google Sheets, producing records that can support advocacy.

Lower barriers to entry and broader volunteer recruitment

This is harder to demonstrate: societies have used the same Google Forms process, often followed by interviews, for years, and we would need other recruitment mechanisms as counter-examples. Acceptance rates are reportedly high, which more effective digital organising may help make possible.

Limits of our study

Our findings rest on three interviews, two of them with SPRH members. They reveal patterns worth taking seriously, not a representative picture of every campus society.

General conclusion and answer to the problématique

How has the digitalisation of organisational work and on-the-ground actions reshaped and impacted volunteer experience in nonprofit social welfare organisations?

In a nutshell, with our premise arguments, our literature review, and our case study, we are able to demonstrate that the use of digital tools by nonprofit social welfare organisations has not only transformed the way they organise, plan, and communicate both internally and externally. It has had a profound and pervasive impact on the very logic that traditionally underpinned these organisations—namely nonprofit models fighting capitalism’s intrinsic damages—and the meaning attached to volunteer work—the solidarity spirit motivations.

Mutual dependency between gains in organisational effectiveness and the pitfalls of alienation from the cause is the fundamental tension that affects volunteer experience, and that is intrinsic to the current nature of the digital world—that is, dominated by market logic. Therefore, if the adoption of digital tools is reportedly seen as both helping and necessary as well as constraining and detrimental, then it is up to the individual actor to gauge the costs and benefits of its practice.

What, then, are the possible paths to overcome this intrinsic mutual dependency embedded in the contemporary form of the corporate-owned digital world? Looking at alternative, collectively owned, and anticapitalist digital tools might be a solution, but this has to come along with a call into question of injunctions for ever-increasing effectiveness and a reconceptualisation of time and the nature of work orientated towards more human usefulness and community.

Opening: the beneficiaries are also deeply impacted by this digital turn

Yet, nonprofit social welfare organisations rely on volunteers, but their work and philosophy is directed at the beneficiaries. Hence, forgetting how this digital turn has impacted them would mean missing the other fundamental aspect of our question.

Here too, positive aspects could be identified, notably enhanced advertising and information access, increased participation and reclaimed services, better accountability through feedback, and an increased number of possible beneficiaries.

However, it can also lead to digital exclusion, a depersonalization and commodification of the service.
